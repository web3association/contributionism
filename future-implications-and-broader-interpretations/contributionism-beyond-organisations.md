# Contributionism beyond organisations

The organisational model described in this resource can be understood as one application of a broader idea: economic arrangements should recognise contribution rather than allow historical asset ownership to determine every future benefit.

This broader interpretation is related to the organisational proposal, but it is not identical to it.



## **The narrower organisational model**

The narrower model concerns:

* Accepted contributions.
* Compensation agreements.
* Governance participation.
* Limits on historical claims.
* Organisational accountability.

Its boundaries are relatively identifiable because an organisation can define membership, contracts and decision-making procedures.



## **The broader economic question**

At the level of an economy, the relevant contributions are much more difficult to define. People benefit from:

* Public infrastructure.
* Scientific knowledge.
* Education.
* Ecosystems.
* Legal institutions.
* Community trust.
* Unpaid care.
* Work performed by previous generations.

These benefits cannot always be attributed to a current organisation or an identifiable individual claimant. A broad contribution-based economy therefore requires additional principles beyond those used for internal organisational accounting.



## **Organisational contribution is not total social contribution**

A person’s paid work does not capture everything they contribute to society. Important activities may occur outside formal organisations, including:

* Caring for children or relatives.
* Supporting neighbours.
* Maintaining community relationships.
* Volunteering.
* Creating openly shared knowledge.
* Protecting local environments.

Conversely, an activity that generates substantial organisational revenue may impose wider social costs. Financial success and social contribution should not be treated as identical.



## **Contribution and need**

Some people cannot provide the types of contribution that markets or organisations reward. Others provide valuable work that is difficult to measure. A broader framework must therefore consider how rights and access arise from need, citizenship, residency, disability, childhood, care obligations, shared ownership and basic human protections. Not every legitimate entitlement needs to be earned through a recorded contribution.



## **Public governance**

Democratic participation ordinarily rests on political membership and legal equality, not on an assessment of economic productivity. Contributionism is not a proposal to give wealthier or more productive citizens greater political voting power. Organisational governance and public governance have different purposes and sources of legitimacy. Temporary residence or changing community membership may affect local eligibility, but those questions should be resolved through appropriate democratic and legal principles.



## **Non-market settings**

Where goods and services are not allocated primarily through market exchange, contribution can still matter. An organisation may use agreed responsibilities, shared access, collective provision, rotating tasks, mutual support or recognition to influence these allocations. Detailed monetary valuations may be unnecessary or inappropriate. The framework’s principles of clarity, accountability and respect for commitments can remain relevant without requiring every benefit to be priced.



## **Avoiding a universal contribution score**

A broad interpretation should not imply that every person needs a permanent, comprehensive contribution rating. Such a system could create surveillance, exclusion, bias, pressure to perform measurable activity, loss of privacy and excessive central control. Different communities and organisations can recognise contribution in different ways. A local assessment should not become a universal judgment of a person’s worth.



## **Defining the broader project honestly**

If Contributionism is extended to land, money, public assets or basic income, those extensions need their own objectives, theoretical arguments, legal proposals, evidence and trade-off analysis. They should not be presented as consequences already established by the organisational model.

This narrower proposal of Contributionism can be evaluated independently of any agreement with a broader and more complete economic philosophy.
