# AI, automation and declining demand for labour

AI, robotics and other forms of automation may change how organisations create value and how people contribute to them. The scale and timing of these changes remain uncertain. Contributionism can be considered against several possible outcomes rather than assume one inevitable future.



## **Automation can complement or replace work**

Technology can:

* Increase worker productivity.
* Remove particular tasks.
* Create new tasks.
* Change the skills required.
* Reduce demand for some occupations.
* Expand demand by lowering costs or enabling new services.

The effect on total labour demand depends on more than technical capability. Costs, regulation, consumer preferences, organisational adoption and new uses of labour also matter.



## **Attribution becomes more difficult**

An AI-assisted output may depend on:

* The person directing the system.
* Software developers.
* Training data.
* Computing infrastructure.
* Organisational processes.
* Earlier research.
* Human review.
* Subsequent implementation.

It may be misleading to attribute the entire result to the person who entered the final instruction. Equally, using an automated tool does not make the operator’s judgment irrelevant. Contribution valuation could examine the actual combination of inputs rather than assuming that visible final actions account for all value.



## **AI as an evaluation tool**

AI may help organisations:

* Summarise contribution records.
* Identify missing evidence.
* Compare work against explicit criteria.
* Detect inconsistencies.
* Support review preparation.

However, it might also:

* Misinterpret context.
* Invent explanations.
* Reproduce historical bias.
* Reward measurable activity over meaningful outcomes.
* Overstate novelty.
* Expose confidential information.

An AI-generated contribution score should not be treated as an objective measurement merely because it is numerical. Consequential assessments require human accountability and a meaningful appeal process.



## **Ownership of automated productive systems**

If an organisation becomes highly automated, a relatively small group may control substantial productive capacity. Temporary historical claims may reduce one source of enduring control, but they do not automatically solve the distribution problem.

For example, if current labour becomes the primary basis for governance while very little labour remains necessary, a small maintenance team could gain control over assets serving a large population. This outcome may be inconsistent with the intended purpose of the organisation. The appropriate response may involve consumer, public or common ownership rather than simply recalculating worker contribution units.



## **The problem of creating work to retain rights**

If rights depend heavily on continuing human activity, participants may have an incentive to preserve unnecessary tasks. An organisation should not require avoidable labour merely to maintain eligibility for benefits or governance. Possible responses include:

* Separating public or member rights from work.
* Providing broader beneficiary representation.
* Using shared ownership arrangements.
* Treating productivity gains as an opportunity for reduced working time.
* Revising eligibility rules transparently.

These responses change the relationship between contribution and entitlement and should be acknowledged as substantive design choices.



## **Distribution beyond participating workers**

If automation substantially reduces the need for paid labour, organisational compensation reform may become less sufficient as a response to inequality. People outside the remaining workforce may need access to income and essential services through other institutions. Possible policy responses include:

* Public services.
* Social insurance.
* Basic income, universal income or other forms of income support.
* Shared public investment funds.
* Broader ownership of productive assets.
* Reduced working time.



## **Meaningful contribution beyond necessity**

People may continue collaborating even where survival depends less on paid work. They may contribute to science, art, education, community life, environmental restoration, recreation and open knowledge.

Transparent priorities, accountable leadership and fair recognition can remain useful in these settings. However, access to basic necessities should not automatically be made conditional on a detailed organisational contribution score.



## **The main implication**

AI, robotics and automation progress strengthens the need to distinguish:

* Who operates a system.
* Who owns it.
* Who benefits from it.
* Who is affected by it.
* Who has authority over its purpose.

The value of human labour could continue to fall due to the growing capabilities of AI and automations. This may result in a falling value for accurately measuring the value and impact of each persons labour. If this is the reality we will soon face, it could make more sense to focus on who owns these productive systems and making sure they are more for the benefit of the public rather than a select few instead of spending large amounts of time trying to compare and evaluate human contributions.

The risks are wealth consolidation will likely increase due the growing capabilities of AI, robotics and automations due to the fact that fewer people could be able to control systems that produce large productive outcomes. Owners that could exert a large amount of influence over a broader population when those systems are only owned and controlled by a small part of the population.

Contributionism is still useful for identifying some of the core problems that can be seen in organisations that exist today, such as perpetual shares of ownership and contributions which are not strongly linked to the issuance of compensation rights. These solutions may still be useful in the future as people will still collaborate together on projects and ideas that are not necessarily focussed on producing any productive economic output. However the full value and impact of adopting temporary shares of ownership and compensation rights that are more strong linked with contribution would be reduced due to the growing prevalence of organisations that are being mostly or fully operated by AI, robotics and automations.
