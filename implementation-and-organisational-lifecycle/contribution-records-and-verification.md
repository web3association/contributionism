# Contribution records and verification

Contribution records document what people have contributed to an organisation, when those contributions were made and how they were recognised. These records can support compensation, governance and organisational learning. They can also benefit contributors beyond their involvement in a particular organisation by helping them demonstrate their skills, achievements and experience. The objective is to create reliable evidence that remains useful over time, not to record every activity or introduce unnecessary surveillance.



## **Why lasting records matter**

The full impact of a contribution may not be apparent when it is made. A person might develop a product, establish an important relationship or introduce a process whose value becomes clear only years later. Without reliable records, their role may be forgotten, disputed or attributed to someone else. Lasting records help preserve evidence of those contributions. They make it easier to:

* Recognise historical contributions accurately.
* Understand how later outcomes became possible.
* Review whether agreed compensation has been provided.
* Identify contributions whose impact was initially underestimated.
* Give credit to people who have left the organisation.

The record of a contribution and the rights associated with it are separate things. A compensation or governance right may expire while the historical record remains valuable. Keeping that record does not automatically renew an expired right or create an additional payment obligation.



## **Permanent records and contributor protection**

Where appropriate, contributors should be able to retain durable, verifiable evidence of their contributions throughout their careers. They should not depend entirely on an organisation continuing to exist, retaining its internal systems or agreeing to provide a favourable reference.

Organisations should therefore avoid allowing contribution records to be arbitrarily deleted, rewritten or withheld to disadvantage a contributor. A change in leadership or a disagreement with a former worker should not erase accurate evidence of completed work. Useful protections include:

* Access to relevant personal contribution records.
* Exportable copies or verified summaries.
* Clear correction and dispute procedures.
* Evidence of who verified a record and when.
* Appropriate records of material changes.
* A process for obtaining records after departure or organisational closure.

“Permanent” should describe the aim of preserving useful historical evidence, not an absolute requirement to retain every detail forever. Personal information may need to be corrected, restricted or deleted under applicable law. Sensitive records may also become unnecessary. Organisations should preserve meaningful evidence while applying appropriate privacy, confidentiality and retention rules.



## **How contribution records can be used**



**Proof of contribution**

Verified records can provide evidence that someone performed particular work, supplied an asset or fulfilled an agreed responsibility. This can help resolve disagreements about who contributed what and whether commitments were completed. A record should distinguish a confirmed fact from an opinion about the contribution’s quality or importance.



**Achievements**

Records can help contributors demonstrate completed projects, improvements, discoveries and other accomplishments. Where work was collaborative, records should recognise the relevant team contributions rather than imply that one person produced the entire outcome independently.



**Skills and reputation**

A reliable contribution history can help someone demonstrate their experience when applying for a new role, seeking collaborators or offering professional services. It may reduce dependence on job titles, personal connections or an organisation’s willingness to provide a reference. However, a contribution history is evidence of particular experience, not a complete or permanent judgment of a person’s ability.



**Interests and values**

Contributions can provide some evidence of the activities and causes a person has chosen to support. This may help people find organisations and collaborators with similar interests. These inferences should be made carefully. A person’s work history also reflects available opportunities and practical constraints; it does not reveal everything about their values.



**Historical reflection**

Organisations can use records to understand which contributions helped produce later outcomes. This can support a review of any delayed impact, recognition of overlooked work, improvements to future valuation methods, learnings from successful and unsuccessful projects and for understanding how organisational capabilities developed. Historical review should consider subsequent contributions rather than attributing all later success to the earliest recorded activity.



**Informed decisions by prospective contributors**

Appropriately shared records can help someone considering an organisation understand the work involved and skills required, how teams collaborate, what contributors have achieved and how contributions are recognised. This can make it easier to assess whether the organisation is a suitable place to contribute. Useful information can often be provided through selected examples or anonymised summaries without publishing individual compensation or confidential work.



**Compliance and accountability**

Records may support audits, contractual obligations, funding requirements and regulatory compliance. They can help demonstrate that work occurred, funds were used appropriately or an agreed process was followed. Their usefulness depends on the quality of the evidence and the requirements of the relevant agreement or law.



**Legacy**

Contributors may wish to preserve a long-term account of their work across different organisations. This can document professional achievements, community involvement and the development of their skills over time. A lasting record can preserve recognition even after the associated financial and governance rights have ended.



## **Separate contributions, rights, payments and decisions**

A reliable system should distinguish four types of record:

* **Contribution records** - These describe what was provided, by whom and when.
* **Rights records** - These identify the compensation or governance rights created under an agreement.
* **Payment records** - These show what has been paid or provided and which obligations those payments satisfy.
* **Decision records** - These explain approvals, valuations, amendments and dispute outcomes. Keeping these categories separate prevents misunderstandings. Recording a contribution value does not automatically mean that the organisation owes that amount, and keeping a contribution record does not mean its associated voting rights remain active.



## **What contribution records should include**

The required detail depends on the contribution and the purpose of the record. Useful fields may include:

* A unique reference.
* The contributor’s identity.
* The contribution type.
* A description of the work, asset or resource provided.
* Relevant dates.
* The person or body accepting the contribution.
* The applicable agreement.
* Supporting evidence.
* The valuation method, where relevant.
* Verification status.
* Associated rights.
* Corrections or unresolved disputes.

Labour records may include outputs, responsibilities and relevant feedback. Financial contributions may rely more heavily on agreements and transaction records. Organisations should collect enough information to support sound decisions without requiring contributors to document every minor action.



## **Verification and assessment**

Verification asks whether the recorded contribution occurred and whether the supporting information is reliable. Assessment asks how the contribution should be valued or recognised. These are different questions.

An organisation may be able to verify that someone created a software library without knowing precisely how much value it will generate. Verification methods can include:

* Payment receipts.
* Accepted work outputs.
* Signed agreements.
* Peer confirmation.
* Customer acceptance.
* Managerial approval.
* Independent review.

The record should indicate what has actually been verified. A payment receipt proves that a transaction occurred; it does not necessarily prove that the associated work was valuable or completed to the required standard.



## **Contributor access and portability**

Contributors should have a practical way to obtain appropriate evidence of their work, including after leaving. A portable record might contain:

* A description of the contribution.
* The relevant dates.
* The contributor’s role.
* Confirmation from an authorised verifier.
* Approved links or supporting materials.
* Any important qualifications or limitations.

Portability gives people greater control over evidence of their contributions. It does not automatically transfer ownership of the organisation’s intellectual property, customer information or confidential materials. The organisation should therefore distinguish between sharing evidence of work and sharing the underlying work itself.



## **Corrections and disputed records**

Records can contain mistakes. Assessments can also change when new evidence emerges. Contributors should be able to request corrections, provide additional evidence and challenge materially unfair descriptions. For significant changes, an audit trail can identify:

* What changed.
* Why it changed.
* Who approved the change.
* When it occurred.
* Whether the contributor disputes it.

A record should not be silently altered in a way that misrepresents someone’s work. Equally, an inaccurate record should not become impossible to correct merely because it was intended to be permanent.



## **Privacy and selective sharing**

Contribution records do not need to be public to be useful. Different information may be suitable for different audiences:

* The contributor.
* Their team.
* Compensation reviewers.
* Auditors.
* Prospective employers.
* The public.

Access should be proportionate to the purpose. Sensitive information may include personal circumstances, compensation, customer data, confidential designs and commercial negotiations. Where possible, contributors should be able to share a verified summary without exposing unnecessary underlying information.



## **Technology and long-term preservation**

Spreadsheets, databases and existing organisational systems may be sufficient. Whatever technology is used, the organisation should consider:

* Backups.
* Export formats.
* Access controls.
* Version history.
* Verification evidence.
* Retention requirements.
* Access after closure.

Blockchain or similar systems may help establish that a record existed at a particular time or has not been altered. They do not automatically establish that the original information was accurate or that the contribution was fairly assessed. Public, immutable storage can also create privacy and correction difficulties. Technology should support reliable evidence and contributor protection rather than make records unnecessarily public or inflexible.



## **The intended outcome**

Contribution records should serve both the organisation and the contributor. They should help the organisation make informed decisions while giving individuals reliable evidence that does not disappear when their role, leadership or workplace changes. The central distinction is that rights can be temporary while evidence of contribution remains lasting, verifiable and useful.
