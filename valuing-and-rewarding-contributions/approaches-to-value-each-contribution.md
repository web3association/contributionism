# Approaches to value each contribution

Contribution valuation helps an organisation decide how to recognise work and resources. This often does not mean discovering an exact, universally correct price for every contribution.



## Understanding each phase of a contribution



**1. Inviting contribution**

* **Identifying** – Identify the contributions the organisation needs now and in the future. Review these regularly as priorities and circumstances change.
* **Encouraging** – Clearly communicate the organisation’s mission, priorities and rewards to attract the people and resources it needs.
* **Accepting** – Decide how to select contributors and accept their contributions, using processes suited to the organisation’s needs.



**2. Measuring contribution**

* **Recording** – Keep records of contributions to support verification, evaluation and future decision-making.
* **Verifying** – Check that contributions are genuine and accurately recorded, especially when they influence rewards or decision-making rights.
* **Evaluating** – Assess the quality, impact and value of contributions, using internal comparisons and relevant external examples.



**3. Rewarding contribution**

* **Paying** – Fairly compensate contributors through appropriate arrangements, such as income, bonuses, dividends or investment returns.
* **Reflecting** – Review past contributions to recognise their long-term impact and consider additional rewards where appropriate.
* **Appreciating** – Recognise and celebrate contributions through regular updates, events or other approaches that suit the organisation and its contributors.



## **Different meanings of value**

An organisation may be interested in several different quantities:

* The market price of an input.
* The cost of producing it.
* The cost of replacing it.
* The benefit it creates.
* The amount the organisation agreed to pay.
* The financial exposure accepted by the contributor.

These quantities can differ substantially. A useful contribution may have been inexpensive to produce. An expensive project may generate little benefit. The organisation should state which meaning of value is being used for each decision.



## **Valuing financial capital**

The nominal amount of a financial investment is usually straightforward to record. A £100,000 investment provides £100,000 at the time of transfer. However, the appropriate financial return is not determined by that amount alone. It can depend on timing, repayment conditions, security, probability of loss, opportunity cost, alternative financing options and governance protections.

The amount invested is known. Its eventual impact and the price of its risk are often not as easily understood.



## **Valuing physical assets**

Physical assets can be assessed using:

* Purchase price.
* Market value.
* Replacement cost.
* Rental value.
* Expected useful life.
* Condition.
* Resale value.

The agreement must distinguish transferring ownership from lending or leasing the asset. An organisation should not credit the full ownership value of a machine when it has only received temporary use, unless the valuation method explicitly justifies that treatment.



## **Valuing intellectual assets**

Intellectual property can be difficult to value before commercial use. Possible approaches include:

* Comparable licences.
* Development cost.
* Expected economic benefits.
* Negotiated royalties.
* Milestone-based payments.

Development cost is evidence of effort and resources used, but it is not necessarily a reliable measure of market value. Licensing also differs from transferring ownership.



## **Valuing labour**

Labour valuation may consider agreed working time, role requirements, market rates, responsibility, performance or long-term impact.

The full effect of work may take time to become visible. Some effects may never be separately measurable. Market rates are useful reference points, but they also reflect bargaining power, scarcity and institutional conditions. They should not automatically be treated as a complete measure of contribution.



## **Joint production**

Organisational outcomes often arise from complementary work.

A sales contract may depend on product quality, reputation, technical support and previous customer relationships. Crediting the entire value to the final salesperson would overlook those inputs.

It cannot be assumed that all individual contributions can be independently added to reconstruct total organisational value.



## **Selecting a practical method**

Possible approaches include:

* Agreed role bands.
* Standardised hourly or daily rates.
* Peer review.
* Managerial assessment.
* Team outcomes.
* Milestone completion.
* Retrospective impact review.
* A combination of methods.

The method should be sufficiently reliable for its purpose and proportionate to the stakes.



## **Valuation is not an automatic debt**

Recording a contribution value does not necessarily create an obligation to pay that amount. The organisation must separately define how valuation affects:

* Current compensation.
* Future compensation rights.
* Governance influence through voting power.
* Recognition.
* Contractual entitlements.

Without that distinction, a valuation system can create misunderstandings and financially unrealistic expectations.
