# Risk, deferred payment and return expectations

People who accept financial uncertainty may reasonably expect different compensation from people receiving secure, immediate payment. Contributionism recognises this distinction while requiring care in how risk is measured and rewarded.



## **Identify what is actually at risk**

For a capital investor, the exposure might include:

* Some or all of the money invested.
* Loss in an asset’s value.
* Foregone use of an asset.
* Recovery costs.
* Delayed access to funds.

For a worker, exposure might include:

* Lawfully deferred compensation.
* A contractually contingent reward.
* Reduced pay accepted in expectation of future compensation rights to benefit from any surplus.
* Lost opportunities associated with joining a risky organisation.

These risks are not identical. A worker who receives their full agreed pay has not accepted the same unpaid-compensation exposure as a worker who receives nothing. Job insecurity can still matter, but it should be identified separately rather than counted as though the entire salary remained unpaid.



## **Probability, severity and timing**

Risk assessment should consider:

* The probability of non-payment.
* The amount that may be lost.
* Possible recovery on failure.
* The time before payment.
* The uncertainty of the estimate.

The probability that an organisation survives is not necessarily the same as the probability that a particular claim will be paid in full. A company can survive while restructuring debts or failing to meet expected returns.



## **A simplified probability example**

Suppose a contributor is exposed to losing £70,000 and has a 90% probability of receiving the entire agreed payout.

Ignoring time, costs and recovery on failure, a payout of approximately £77,778 in the successful case produces expected proceeds of £70,000:

**0.9 × £77,778 ≈ £70,000**

This is a simplified expected-value calculation, not a complete recommendation for fair compensation. It differs from simply adding 10% to the amount at risk.



## **Why simple multipliers are limited**

Multipliers can help illustrate risk, but practical estimates are uncertain. Additional concerns include:

* Failure probabilities may be poorly known.
* Losses may be correlated.
* Investors may require liquidity.
* Workers may be unable to diversify their exposure.
* Inflation and time affect the value of later payment.
* The organisation may lack the future capacity to generate the proposed return.

A nominally appropriate multiplier could still produce an economically unworkable agreement.



## **Market capacity and affordability**

The organisation should examine whether its plausible future revenue and surplus can support the proposed rewards. A business with a limited addressable market cannot make every early contribution financeable merely by assigning it a large multiplier. The parties may instead need to:

* Reduce costs.
* Change the product.
* Seek different financing.
* Limit the project.
* Accept lower financial expectations.
* Decide not to proceed.

Personal enthusiasm can justify voluntarily accepting lower returns. It should not conceal unrealistic promises.



## **Risk is not the same as complexity**

A technically complex organisation is not necessarily financially riskier than a simple one.

Complexity may justify higher compensation because it requires scarce expertise or greater responsibility. Risk concerns uncertain loss.

Scoring both factors can be useful, but combining them into one multiplier requires justification and may count the same difficulty more than once.



## **Setting terms in advance**

Where possible, risk-related terms should be agreed before the outcome is known.

Retrospectively declaring a successful project “extremely risky” can allow insiders to award themselves excessive claims.

Later adjustments may be appropriate when genuinely new information emerges, but the process should be protected against self-serving changes.



## **Legal and personal safeguards**

Deferred wages and similar arrangements are subject to employment and other legal requirements.

Workers should not be pressured to finance an organisation through unlawful non-payment.

They should also receive clear information about the possibility of loss and the concentration of their livelihood and savings in the same organisation.
