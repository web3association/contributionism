# Why limiting historical rights is not enough

Contributionism addresses both the duration of rights and the rules through which contributors receive them. An organisation can limit historical ownership without treating new contributions fairly. It can also distribute rights widely while allowing those rights to continue indefinitely. A complete proposal must address both questions:

1. When should existing rights end?
2. Which contributions should create new rights?



## **Temporary rights can still be distributed unfairly**

Suppose an organisation issues shares that expire after ten years. The founder and investors receive those shares, but workers receive only fixed pay. Workers have no established entitlement to additional rewards when their contributions substantially improve the organisation.

The expiry date limits how long the original holders benefit. It does not establish how other contributions will be recognised during those ten years or who receives rights afterwards. The organisation could simply issue another allocation to the same favoured group. Temporary rights therefore need to be accompanied by consistent eligibility and issuance rules.



## **Broad participation does not resolve perpetuity**

Now suppose an organisation gives shares to every worker and investor. This creates a stronger connection between contribution and ownership. However, if every allocation lasts indefinitely, the organisation must still address the continuing influence and financial claims of historical holders. A person may leave, sell their shares or pass them to an heir while the rights continue. Broad issuance improves access to rights. It does not answer how long those rights should remain active.



## **Compensation and governance require different rules**

The connection between contribution and compensation can often be applied more consistently across similar contributions. For example, workers performing comparable roles under comparable conditions should normally have access to the same compensation and surplus-sharing arrangements.

Governance is more decision-specific. A contribution may justify:

* Participation in workplace decisions.
* A vote in leadership selection.
* Protection against changes to a financial agreement.
* Access to information.
* No authority over unrelated technical or operational matters.

Contributionism should therefore distinguish:

> _**A consistent entitlement to appropriate compensation arrangements from an entitlement to every governance decision.**_

Not everyone needs identical authority. However, those differences should still have a clear rationale and should not leave contributors without meaningful protection.



## **Existing wages are already compensation rights**

Workers in conventional organisations do not necessarily lack financial rights altogether. Wages, contractual bonuses and other benefits are compensation rights. The concern is more specific: additional rewards associated with organisational success may depend primarily on ownership status rather than consistent treatment of contributions.

Similarly, workers may have statutory consultation or representation rights without possessing shareholder votes. The documentation should recognise those protections while examining where important gaps remain.



## **From discretionary recognition to established entitlement**

An organisation may voluntarily reward a valuable contributor. That is different from having rules under which comparable contributions are consistently entitled to consideration and benefit. A contribution-linked arrangement should make clear:

* Which contributions qualify.
* Which rights they create.
* How those rights are calculated.
* When they arise.
* How long they last.
* How they can be enforced.

The objective is to make fair treatment part of the organisation’s structure rather than something contributors must repeatedly request from those already in control.
