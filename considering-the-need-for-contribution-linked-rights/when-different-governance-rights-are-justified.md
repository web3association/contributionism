# When different governance rights are justified

Equal treatment does not require every contributor to have identical authority over every decision. Some differences in governance rights are reasonable. Others merely preserve an established group’s power. An organisation should be able to explain the distinction.



## **Start with the decision, not the person’s status**

The first question should be:

> _**What decision is being made, and who has a relevant responsibility, interest or need for protection?**_

This is more useful than asking whether a particular person should receive all governance rights or none. A technical team may reasonably select its tools without seeking a vote from every donor. A lender may require protection against additional borrowing without controlling employee schedules.



## **Possible reasons for differentiated authority**

Differences may be justified by:

* Specialist responsibility.
* A defined leadership mandate.
* Protection of a financial commitment.
* A public or charitable purpose.
* Professional obligations.
* Creative direction.
* A proportionate onboarding period.
* The need for timely operational decisions.

The scope of the authority should correspond to the reason. A valid reason for control over one subject does not automatically justify control over unrelated matters.



## **Family organisations and creative groups**

A family-operated business may seek continuity of expertise and identity.

A band may wish to preserve artistic direction among its performers.

These concerns may justify specific leadership or creative rights. They do not automatically justify excluding support workers from every decision affecting their conditions or compensation. The organisation should distinguish:

* Strategic or creative direction.
* Daily management.
* Workplace policies.
* Financial rights.
* Leadership accountability.

This allows continuity without treating all non-core contributors as having no legitimate voice.



## **Delegation is different from exclusion**

An eligible contributor may prefer to delegate decisions to an elected leader. That is different from never having a right to influence who receives the authority or how it is reviewed.

Likewise, not wanting to vote frequently does not necessarily mean consenting to permanent exclusion. Organisations can reduce governance burdens through delegation without removing the underlying accountability relationship.



## **Compensation for narrower governance rights**

Some contributors may prefer greater financial compensation and fewer governance responsibilities. An organisation may offer such an arrangement where lawful and appropriate. However:

* The agreement should be informed and explicit.
* The additional benefit should be clearly defined.
* Essential legal protections remain.
* Access to complaints and enforcement should remain.
* Payment should not automatically justify arbitrary authority over working conditions.

There is no established universal percentage that fairly compensates for reduced governance rights. A proposed bonus should be evaluated in context rather than treated as a standard price for giving up influence.



## **Documenting an exclusion**

Where an otherwise relevant contributor does not receive a particular governance right, the organisation should state:

1. Which right is restricted.
2. The reason.
3. Who exercises it instead.
4. The duration of the arrangement.
5. The protections available to the excluded contributor.
6. How the arrangement can be reviewed.

A general statement that “the founders prefer control” is not equivalent to a decision-specific rationale.



## **Reviewing the rationale**

Circumstances change. A restriction appropriate during an initial development phase may become unnecessary once the organisation is established. A seasonal worker may become a long-term contributor. A specialist responsibility may disappear. A founder’s initial mandate may be completed. Governance differences should therefore be reviewable rather than automatically becoming permanent features.



## **The intended standard**

Contributionism should favour appropriate participation while allowing justified differences. The standard is not:

> _**Every contribution receives identical authority.**_

It is:

> _**Relevant contributors should have appropriate influence and protection, and restrictions should be specific, transparent, proportionate and accountable.**_
