# Misaligned incentives when rights are separated from contribution

Separating ownership from current contribution can create incentives that work against consistent recognition, accurate evaluation and long-term organisational development. These incentives are not the only influences on behaviour. Owners and managers may act responsibly, and market competition can encourage good treatment. Nevertheless, organisational rules should account for conflicts of interest rather than rely on them being overcome voluntarily.



## **Incentives to limit financial participation**

Existing owners benefit from an organisation attracting capable contributors. They may also benefit financially from providing no more compensation or ownership than is needed to recruit and retain those contributors. The result can be a tension between:

* Rewarding contribution according to the organisation’s stated principles.
* Preserving the returns of existing rights holders.

A contributor may generate substantial value without having a corresponding claim if they lack the bargaining power to demand one. Contribution-linked rules make the basis of compensation more explicit and reduce reliance on individual negotiation.



## **Incentives to limit governance participation**

Existing decision-makers may value worker knowledge while resisting formal rights that would make their own authority contestable. They may favour consultation that can be ignored over participation that creates enforceable influence. Consultation can be useful, but contributors should understand the difference between:

* Being invited to give an opinion.
* Having a right to receive a response.
* Having a vote.
* Having approval or consent authority.

Describing all four as “participation” can obscure meaningful differences in power.



## **Weak incentives to create contributor-controlled evidence**

Organisations already have reasons to assess performance. They need to allocate work, identify problems and retain capable people. However, they may have less incentive to create transparent, verifiable and portable records that strengthen a contributor’s independent bargaining position. Such records could help people:

* Demonstrate their achievements.
* Identify inconsistent compensation.
* Negotiate higher rewards.
* Obtain work elsewhere.

Where the benefits primarily accrue to contributors while the organisation bears the cost, investment in those records may be limited. The concern is not that conventional organisations never evaluate work. It is that evaluation can remain focused on managerial control rather than giving contributors reliable evidence and recourse.



## **Incentives can favour short-term financial outcomes**

Owners and executives may face pressure to improve near-term financial results. Depending on their incentives, this can encourage:

* Underinvestment.
* Excessive cost-cutting.
* Reduced maintenance.
* Lower spending on training.
* Decisions that shift costs onto workers or communities.

Practices such as outsourcing or share repurchases are not inherently harmful. Their effects depend on circumstances and what alternatives are being displaced. The relevant concern is whether the people making decisions receive benefits while important longer-term costs fall on others.



## **Lack of shared benefit can weaken support for improvement**

Workers may resist an innovation if its main consequences are job loss or increased workload while the financial benefit goes to owners. That response may be rational from their perspective. A contribution-linked organisation could improve alignment through:

* Shared productivity gains.
* Reduced working time.
* Retraining.
* Transition support.
* Transparent decisions about reinvestment and distributions.

However, contributor ownership does not automatically remove resistance to change. Current members can also prioritise their own positions over future organisational needs.



## **Better rights can improve incentives, but they require safeguards**

Giving contributors a stake in outcomes can support better alignment. It can also create new incentives to:

* Exaggerate contribution.
* Manipulate valuations.
* Protect an advantageous formula.
* Exclude new participants.
* Prioritise payments before rights expire.

Contributionism therefore needs both rights and accountability. The intended improvement is not that contributors become free of self-interest. It is that organisational rules make interests more visible, distribute benefits more consistently and provide mechanisms for handling conflict.
