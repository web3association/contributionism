# Governance problems when contribution does not create influence

People can be responsible for operating an organisation without having an established way to influence those who govern it. This can occur even where the organisation’s ownership rights are temporary. The problem is therefore not only how long governance rights last. It is also whether relevant contributions create an appropriate route to decision-making authority.



## **Leadership can become difficult to challenge**

In conventional corporate governance, shareholders generally influence board appointments, and the board oversees executive leadership. Workers without relevant voting or representation rights may have little authority over that process. Leadership can remain in place even when current contributors believe it is:

* Performing poorly.
* Ignoring operational problems.
* Damaging workplace culture.
* Failing to invest in the future.
* Acting against the organisation’s stated priorities.

Workers may attempt to persuade leaders informally, organise collectively or leave. These options can be important, but they are not the same as an established governance mechanism.



## **Stagnation can continue despite changes in contribution**

An organisation may develop new activities, recruit different expertise and become dependent on a different group of people. Without contribution-linked eligibility, those changes need not affect who controls leadership selection.

The organisation can therefore become less responsive to the knowledge and preferences of its current contributors. Long-serving leadership is not inherently stagnant. A capable leader may retain broad support for decades. The concern is leadership that persists because contributors lack a practical route to review or replace it.



## **Influence depends on access and goodwill**

A worker may be heard because they have a good relationship with a founder or manager. Another worker with equally relevant knowledge may have less access. Informal influence can therefore depend on:

* Personal relationships.
* Status.
* Confidence.
* Proximity to leadership.
* Willingness to challenge authority.

These factors do not necessarily identify the people with the most relevant knowledge. Formal participation rights can reduce the need for contributors to rely on personal access or goodwill.



## **Leaving is not an adequate answer to every disagreement**

A contributor may support most of an organisation’s activities while wanting to change a particular policy or leader. Starting a new organisation can be a disproportionate response. It may also be impractical where the existing organisation depends on:

* Large infrastructure.
* Network effects.
* Extensive customer relationships.
* Economies of scale.
* Licences.
* Specialist assets.

A credible internal route to change can allow contributors to improve an organisation rather than abandon it.



## **Existing decision-makers may resist sharing authority**

Giving new contributors governance rights can reduce the relative influence of existing rights holders. Those holders may therefore have an incentive to issue only as much authority as is necessary to recruit or retain people. This creates a conflict between:

* Recognising the role of current contributors.
* Preserving the position of existing decision-makers.

Contribution-linked eligibility rules can reduce that conflict by establishing the conditions in advance. Those rules must themselves be protected against arbitrary amendment or exclusion.



## **Financial fairness does not remove every need for voice**

An organisation might provide generous compensation while excluding workers from decisions affecting their work. That may still create problems concerning:

* Safety.
* Professional autonomy.
* Working hours.
* Treatment by managers.
* Organisational purpose.
* Job security.
* Respect and dignity.

Additional compensation does not automatically resolve these concerns. The relevant question is which forms of participation and protection are appropriate, not whether higher pay can substitute for every governance right.



## **Contributor governance is not unrestricted worker control**

Workers do not necessarily have exclusive authority over every decision. Consumers, recipients, investors, communities and public bodies may also have legitimate interests. Contributionism can help with connecting authority to the decisions being made. For example:

* Workers may govern working methods.
* Members may select representatives.
* Investors may hold narrowly defined financial protections.
* Public authorities may determine a public service mandate.

The objective is justified and accountable authority, not an assumption that one contributor group should control everything.



## **The Contributionist response**

An organisation should establish routes through which relevant contributors can:

* Obtain information.
* Raise proposals.
* Select or review leadership.
* Participate in decisions affecting their work.
* Challenge misuse of authority.

Where these rights are restricted, the organisation should explain the restriction and provide appropriate protections.
