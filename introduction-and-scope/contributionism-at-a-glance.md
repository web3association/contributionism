# Contributionism at a glance

Contributionism is a proposed economic model for organisations. It concerns how organisations are owned, governed and how the people who contribute to them are rewarded.

Its central aim is to maintain a meaningful connection between contribution, compensation and decision-making authority as an organisation develops.

An organisation may operate for decades or centuries. During that time, its contributors, activities and sources of value can change substantially. Contributionism asks whether rights created by historical contributions should continue indefinitely, or whether those rights should eventually end so that the organisation remains responsive to the people that are still contributing towards it.



## **The problem Contributionism seeks to address**

Many companies issue shares that have no predetermined expiry date. Depending on their class and the organisation’s governing documents, those shares can provide compensation and governance rights for as long as they remain outstanding.

These arrangements can be useful. They allow investors and founders to accept uncertainty in exchange for participation in future success. They can also reward work that cannot be fully compensated when it is performed.

However, enduring share ownership can create a growing separation between:

* The people currently contributing to an organisation.
* The people receiving its financial returns.
* The people entitled to control its direction.

An early contributor may continue receiving financial benefits long after their involvement has ended. Shares may also be sold or inherited, transferring financial and governance rights to people who did not make the original contribution.

Meanwhile, later contributors may receive wages or other fixed payments without a corresponding right to participate in the organisation’s future success or governance.

Contributionism treats this potential separation as an organisational design problem.



## **The proposed response**

Contributionism proposes that organisations should connect rights more systematically to contributions and prevent those rights from automatically providing unlimited, perpetual claims. The framework has three central features.



**Rights with defined limits**

Rights arising from a contribution should have clearly specified limits.

Governance rights may expire after an agreed period or end when a relevant condition is met. Financial arrangements may provide participation for a defined period, a defined total return or another explicitly limited entitlement.

An important distinction is that limiting future participation does not necessarily mean cancelling a payment that has already become legally owed.



**Contribution-linked rights**

Organisations should apply clear and consistent rules when determining which contributions receive compensation rights and which receive governance rights.

Comparable contributions should not receive fundamentally different treatment merely because one person is an established owner and another is not.



**Organisational self-determination**

Contributionism does not prescribe one voting system, compensation formula or working environment. An organisation might choose:

* Equal voting rights for eligible workers.
* Voting power partly related to contribution value.
* Elected leadership with defined responsibilities.
* Relatively equal compensation and extensive shared benefits.
* More differentiated compensation based on performance and impact.
* Worker, consumer, donor or other appropriate governance arrangements.

An organisation must make these choices themselves and make them transparent and consistent.



## **Compensation and governance are different**

A right to receive payment is not the same as a right to make decisions.

A contributor may need protection for an unpaid financial claim without needing authority over daily operations. Conversely, a current worker may reasonably participate in workplace governance even after receiving full payment for their recent work.

Contributionism therefore distinguishes economic entitlements from decision-making authority, even when an organisation chooses to combine them in one instrument.



## **What Contributionism does not guarantee**

Temporary rights do not automatically produce fair valuations, competent leadership or a successful organisation. Contributionism still requires practical answers to questions such as:

* Who evaluates contributions?
* How are disagreements resolved?
* How are investors and workers protected?
* What happens when the organisation cannot afford the expected rewards?
* How are current contributors prevented from unfairly changing historical commitments?

The framework should therefore be understood as a proposal for organising rights and responsibilities, not as a guarantee that conflicts or economic uncertainty disappear.
