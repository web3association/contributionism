# Public ownership and common assets

Public ownership and common ownership concern resources whose purpose extends beyond the private benefit of a changing group of contributors.

Contributionism can inform how people participate in their operation, but it does not automatically determine who owns those resources.



## **Public ownership**

Public ownership generally means that assets or organisational interests are held by a government or another public body. Publicly owned organisations may provide infrastructure, healthcare, education, utilities, transport and cultural services. Their purpose and obligations arise from public authority and applicable law. Current workers do not automatically acquire a right to sell public assets merely because their labour helps maintain or improve them.



## **Worker participation within public organisations**

Workers can participate in public organisations through:

* Workplace councils.
* Elected representatives.
* Professional committees.
* Delegated operational authority.
* Participation in leadership selection where permitted.

Such arrangements can improve the use of practical knowledge without replacing public accountability. The organisation should distinguish:

* Public decisions about purpose and access.
* Professional decisions about service delivery.
* Workplace decisions.
* Financial decisions subject to public budgets and law.



## **Compensation in public organisations**

Public workers can receive compensation that recognises contribution, responsibility and performance. However, the concept of distributable surplus may be inappropriate or restricted. An unspent public budget is not necessarily a profit available for private distribution. It may be required for future services, reserves or return to the public authority. Contribution records can support recognition and evaluation without creating private ownership claims over public resources.



## **Changes in ownership**

Records of historical work do not, by themselves, establish who should receive an organisation’s assets if public ownership changes. Any privatisation, transfer or restructuring raises separate questions about:

* Public consent.
* Valuation.
* Legal authority.
* Service obligations.
* The interests of taxpayers and users.
* Treatment of workers.
* Future access.

Contribution records may inform a transition, but they cannot substitute for those decisions.



## **Common ownership**

Common ownership concerns resources held or governed for a community rather than primarily for individual financial return. Examples might include:

* Shared land.
* Community facilities.
* Water resources.
* Digital infrastructure.
* Open knowledge resources.

The relevant community and the rules governing access must be defined. “Common” does not mean that access is necessarily unrestricted or that no one is responsible for maintenance.



## **Separating resource governance from organisational governance**

A community can govern a resource without governing every organisation that uses it. For example, a community body might determine the conditions under which a workshop can use a shared building. The workshop’s contributors can then govern their internal operations within those conditions. This separation can make responsibilities clearer:

* The community protects the resource and its public purpose.
* The organisation manages its own work.
* Agreements define access, payment, maintenance and acceptable use.



## **Historical contributions to common assets**

People who establish or maintain a common resource may deserve compensation and recognition. However, rewarding those contributions should not automatically convert a shared resource into an indefinitely extractive private claim. The arrangement should state:

* What the contributor is entitled to receive.
* How maintenance is funded.
* Whether usage charges are permitted.
* Who can modify the rules.
* How the resource’s shared purpose is protected.



## **Relationship to Contributionism**

Contributionism can support transparent compensation and accountable participation within public and common arrangements.

It should not be used to imply that every public right must arise from a measured personal contribution. Citizenship, residency, need and public mandate can provide independent grounds for participation and protection.
