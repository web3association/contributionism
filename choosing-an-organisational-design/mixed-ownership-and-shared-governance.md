# Mixed ownership and shared governance

An organisation may have several groups with legitimate interests in its activities. Workers, consumers, donors, investors and communities can each contribute something important. The challenge is to give those interests appropriate protection without making every decision unnecessarily complex.



## **Mixed ownership and mixed governance are different**

Mixed ownership means that more than one group holds legally recognised ownership interests. Mixed governance means that decision-making responsibilities are shared among different groups. An organisation can have mixed governance without mixed ownership.

For example, workers may govern ordinary operations while consumers elect a service-quality committee and a lender holds consent rights over specified financial transactions.

Conversely, several groups can hold financial interests while ordinary management remains delegated to one leadership team.



## **Potential benefits**

Shared governance can:

* Improve information available to decision-makers.
* Build trust.
* Protect against one group exploiting another.
* Make organisational priorities more representative.
* Support long-term relationships.

A community bank, for example, may benefit from both professional operational knowledge and community representation.



## **Potential difficulties**

Mixed arrangements can also create:

* Overlapping authority.
* Slower decisions.
* Conflicting priorities.
* Unclear accountability.
* Disputes about relative voting weight.

These problems are especially likely when all groups are given broad authority over the same decisions without a clear process for resolving disagreement.



## **Decision-specific authority**

One approach is to divide responsibility by subject. For example:

| **Decision area**       | **Possible primary participants**                  |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Daily working methods   | Relevant workers and managers                      |
| Service priorities      | Consumer or recipient representatives              |
| Employment policies     | Workers and authorised leadership                  |
| Restricted donation use | Governing body within the donation terms           |
| Major borrowing         | Governing body, with applicable lender protections |
| Use of community assets | Relevant public or community authority             |

These are just example suggestions. The appropriate allocation depends on the organisation and legal setting.



## **Representative governance**

Another approach is a board containing representatives from several groups. The governing documents should specify:

* How each group selects representatives.
* The number of seats.
* Terms of office.
* Duties.
* Conflict-of-interest rules.
* Which matters require additional consent.

Representatives should understand whether they act solely as advocates for their group or owe duties to the organisation as a whole. Legal duties may constrain the answer.



## **Separate approval requirements**

Some decisions may require approval from more than one group. For example, changing worker membership rights might require worker approval, while selling a community asset might require public consent.

Such protections can prevent unilateral action, but excessive veto rights can produce deadlock. The organisation should establish escalation, mediation and fallback procedures.



## **Delegated rights and protected rights**

A distinction is needed between authority that the main governing group may revoke and rights protected by an agreement or constitution.

If consumer participation exists only at the discretion of worker leadership, consumers may have less protection than they expect.

A mixed-governance arrangement should therefore state which responsibilities are:

* Informally consulted.
* Delegated and revocable.
* Contractually protected.
* Constitutionally reserved.
* Required by law.



## **Choosing the simplest adequate arrangement**

The objective is not to maximise the number of groups voting on every issue. It is to ensure that relevant interests are represented and protected at a proportionate administrative cost.

A single primary governing group with targeted protections may be sufficient. In other circumstances, genuine multi-stakeholder ownership or representation may be preferable.

Mixed ownership and mixed governance models could increase the overall complexity of operating an organisation. Although this might be true, whether these approaches are suitable or not is ultimately up to the contributors involved to decide themselves.
