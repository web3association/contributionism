# Contributions deserve rewards but not perpetual rights

Conventional shares perform useful functions. They can attract capital, share uncertainty, reward early work and protect long-term commitments. Any alternative must address those functions.

However, establishing that a contribution deserves a reward does not, by itself, establish that the resulting financial and governance rights should never end. Contributionism separates two questions:

1. **What recognition and protection does this contribution justify?**
2. **Why should the associated rights continue indefinitely?**

The following arguments explain why these questions should not be treated as equivalent.



## **“They accepted a serious risk of loss”**

Risk-taking can justify a larger potential reward. An investor may lose capital. A worker may lawfully accept deferred compensation that is never paid. A founder may commit substantial time and resources to an uncertain project. A model that fails to recognise those risks could discourage valuable activity.

However, recognising risk does not automatically determine the appropriate duration of the resulting rights. The parties can consider:

* The amount exposed to loss.
* The likelihood of loss.
* The time before payment.
* Possible recovery on failure.
* The range of successful outcomes.
* The financial return needed to attract the contribution.

These factors can support a substantial return without automatically establishing a permanent claim on every future outcome.



**Financial contribution example**

Suppose an investor provides £100,000 to a highly uncertain startup. For illustration, assume:

* A 1% probability of a successful payout.
* A 99% probability of losing the entire investment.
* No recovery in the unsuccessful cases.

A £10 million payout in the successful case would produce expected proceeds of £100,000:

**1% × £10 million = £100,000**

Under these simplified assumptions, that is nominal expected-value break-even. It is not proof that £10 million is a uniquely fair maximum return. The calculation excludes time, costs, uncertainty about the probabilities and other considerations. An investor may reasonably require different terms. The example nevertheless identifies a useful question: the investment and its risks can be examined when negotiating a defined financial arrangement.

With perpetual shares, the investor may continue benefiting through dividends or share-price appreciation after receiving very substantial returns. There is no built-in point at which the financial right ends because an agreed level of reward has been reached.

Contributionism argues that organisations should explicitly consider limits rather than assume that indefinite participation is the only way to compensate risk. The argument is not that a finite investment mathematically proves a particular cap. It is that risk justifies compensation, while perpetuity requires a further justification.



## **“They took the initiative”**

Initiative can create substantial value. Someone may identify an opportunity, solve a difficult problem, recruit a team or undertake work that others were unwilling to begin. Those contributions deserve recognition.

However, initiative is not exclusive to founders or early investors. Later contributors can also transform an organisation through exceptional judgment and effort. A contribution-based approach should therefore recognise initiative whenever it occurs. The relevant questions are:

* What did the person contribute?
* What value or impact did it create?
* What uncertainty did they accept?
* What compensation is appropriate?

The fact that initiative occurred early does not, by itself, justify giving it unlimited priority over equally valuable initiative occurring later.



## **“They started the organisation”**

Starting an organisation involves more than registering a legal entity. It can require:

* Developing a workable idea.
* Testing demand.
* Building an initial product.
* Bringing people together.
* Securing resources.
* Establishing systems.
* Coordinating activity under uncertainty.

These contributions are important. However, founding is still a set of contributions that can be identified, evaluated and rewarded. Founder status alone does not explain why someone should retain a permanent claim over work performed decades later, including work in activities that did not exist when the organisation began. A founder may receive substantial compensation, longer compensation rights or a defined leadership mandate without receiving unlimited authority by default.



## **“Without their contribution, the organisation would not exist”**

Some early contributions are necessary conditions for the organisation’s existence. That can establish their importance. It does not necessarily establish an unlimited entitlement to every later outcome. Many subsequent contributions may also be necessary for continued success:

* Replacing an obsolete product.
* Preventing insolvency.
* Maintaining critical infrastructure.
* Rebuilding a damaged reputation.
* Adapting to a new market.

An organisation’s continued existence usually depends on a sequence of contributions. Contributionism therefore asks how those contributions should be recognised together rather than treating the earliest necessary contribution as permanently decisive.



## **“Their capital is still supporting the organisation”**

Capital can continue producing benefits after it is initially supplied. Equipment, facilities, intellectual property and working capital may remain important for many years. It would be inaccurate to assume that an investment stops contributing economically as soon as it is made. This can justify long-term financial arrangements. However, the organisation should distinguish:

* Continued use of an asset.
* Compensation already received.
* Maintenance and improvements by later contributors.
* New resources generated by subsequent activity.
* The scope of any original agreement.

Continuing benefit can justify continuing compensation under appropriate terms. It does not automatically establish that the original investor should govern unrelated future activities indefinitely.



## **“Permanent rights are necessary to attract investors”**

Perpetual equity can make some uncertain investments easier to finance. Investors may value:

* Uncapped upside.
* Transferability.
* No fixed repayment deadline.
* Established legal protections.

Contributionism cannot assume that ordinary loans can reproduce all of these features without cost. Alternative arrangements may need to offer substantial contingent returns, transfer opportunities and credible protections. Whether they can attract sufficient capital is an empirical question.

The Contributionist argument is therefore not that perpetual equity serves no purpose. It is that its usefulness should be weighed against its long-term consequences, and alternatives should be tested where they may achieve a better balance.



## **“Stable control is needed to protect the original vision”**

Continuity can be valuable, particularly during a long development process. However, financial ownership and leadership authority do not have to be the same instrument. A defined leadership mandate can provide:

* Authority to execute an agreed plan.
* A sufficiently long term.
* Protection against arbitrary interference.
* Clear limits.
* Review and renewal.
* Removal for serious misconduct or breach.

This can preserve continuity without assuming that the founder must retain permanent control.



## **The central rationale**

Contributionism does not reject rewards for founding, initiative, capital or risk. It challenges the step from:

> _**“This contribution deserves substantial recognition.”**_

to:

> _**“Therefore, the resulting compensation and governance rights received should never lapse or expire.”**_

A contribution may justify a large reward and a long period of protection. The duration, scope and limits of those rights should still be considered and justified more explicitly.
